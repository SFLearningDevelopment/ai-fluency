Using AI responsibly

When to use AI and when not to, protecting confidential data, catching confident mistakes, and reviewing before you act.

The right-hand column is not forbidden. It means AI can help you draft or think, but a qualified person still checks the result before it counts.

Low stakes plus review equals safe

The safe zone is work where you will read the output anyway and a mistake is easy to catch and cheap to fix. Most everyday drafting sits there.

This is the rule with the least flexibility in it. When you paste something into a public AI tool, you have sent it outside the company.

Never paste

  • Customer or employee personal data — names with details, addresses, phone numbers, ID numbers
  • Passwords, API keys, or access credentials of any kind
  • Confidential contracts, pricing, or commercial terms
  • HR records, salary information, performance or disciplinary notes
  • Anything covered by a client NDA
  • Unreleased internal financials or strategy documents

What to do instead

You can almost always get the same help by removing the identifying parts and keeping the shape of the problem.

Do notDo
Paste an employee's full performance record and ask how to phrase the feedback. “How do I phrase feedback about missed deadlines in a supportive way?”
Paste the client contract and ask for a summary. “What sections are usually in a services contract, and what should I look for in each?”
Paste a customer list to tidy the columns. “Give me a Sheets formula to split ‘Name <email>’ into two columns.”
If you are unsure, leave it out

Ask the general version of your question. You lose very little, and once data has been pasted it cannot be taken back.

AI does not signal uncertainty. A wrong answer arrives in the same calm, fluent tone as a right one. There is no wobble in the voice to warn you.

It can invent statistics, dates, names, citations, policy details, and quotes — all sounding entirely plausible.

Where invention is most likely

  • Specific numbers and percentages
  • Dates and historical details
  • Names of people, reports, or studies
  • Legal or regulatory specifics
  • Anything about your company that you did not provide
The test that works

Before using a fact from AI, ask yourself whether you could show someone where it came from. If the answer is no, verify it from a trusted source or leave it out. Confidence in the wording tells you nothing about accuracy.

Asking for sources helps but does not settle it — sources can be invented too. Check that a source exists and says what the AI claims it says.

Everything in this course reduces to one habit: you read it before it goes anywhere. AI helped write it. You are responsible for it.

A short review pass

  1. Are the facts, numbers, and dates correct?
  2. Is the tone right for this person?
  3. Is anything missing that should be there?
  4. Is anything present that should not be, including invented detail?
  5. Does it sound like something you would say?

Thirty seconds. It catches nearly everything that would have been embarrassing.

The closing idea

AI is powerful but not magical. Its usefulness and its safety both depend on how thoughtfully you use it. You stay the expert, and you stay the one who decides.

That is the whole course. Delegate the work that suits it, describe what you want clearly, judge what comes back, and use it responsibly.
