Email with AI

Draft from bullet points, reply with the right tone, fix and translate, and summarise long messages.

Sections complete: 0 of 4

The slowest part of writing an email is usually the first sentence. You already know what you want to say — you just have to turn it into something sendable. That turning is the part you hand over.

How it looks

Write your points as roughly as you like, then ask for the email:

You type I'm an office admin. Turn these into a short, polite email to the housekeeping vendor:
• cleaning was missed on 2nd floor Tuesday
• need it covered this week
• ask them to confirm the day

You get back a complete, professional email. You read it, change anything that is not quite right, and send it.

This is Delegation in practice

You kept the judgement — what to say, who to say it to, whether the tone fits this vendor. You handed over the typing. That split is the point.

Getting a better first draft

  • Say who it goes to: “to a vendor we work with often” reads differently from “to a vendor we are unhappy with”.
  • Say the length: “under 100 words”.
  • Say what must appear: “include the date and ask for confirmation”.

Paste the email you received, say what you want to reply, and ask for a draft. This works even when the incoming message is long or unclear.

You type Here is an email I received. Draft a reply saying we can meet on Thursday but not Wednesday, and ask them to send the agenda in advance. Keep it warm and brief.

Tone is a dial you control

If the draft is not pitched right, name the tone you want and ask again:

You wantYou say
Warmer“make this friendlier, like writing to a colleague”
Firmer“make this firmer — polite but clear that the deadline is not moving”
More formal“rewrite this in formal business English for an external client”
Calmer“the original is rude; keep my reply calm and professional”
Difficult replies are where this helps most

When a message annoys you, your own first draft is usually the one you should not send. Asking for a calm, professional reply that addresses the concern gives you something better than what you would have written while irritated.

Write your reply however it comes out. Then ask for a clean-up before it goes anywhere:

You type Fix the grammar and make this sound professional. Keep it short and keep my meaning.

Ask it to keep your meaning. Without that, a clean-up can quietly change what you were trying to say.

Working across languages

  • Write in your own language and ask for the English version.
  • Paste an English email and ask for it in your language so you are sure you understood it.
  • Ask: “does this sound polite in English?” — useful when a direct translation lands harsher than you intended.
Read the cleaned-up version

A polished draft is easy to trust because it looks finished. Check that the facts, names, dates, and amounts survived the rewrite unchanged.

A long, rambling email with the important thing buried in paragraph six is a good use of AI. Paste it and ask for what you actually need:

You type Summarise this email in 3 bullet points, and list anything I need to do with its deadline.

Asking for action items separately is what makes the summary useful rather than just shorter.

Then check it

Summaries lose things. A date, a condition, a name, a “only if” — these are exactly the details that get dropped, and exactly the details that matter.

The habit worth building

Scan the original for numbers and dates, and confirm each one appears in the summary. It takes seconds and it catches the failure that costs the most.

If something is missing, say so: “you left out the deadline — redo the summary and include all dates.”
