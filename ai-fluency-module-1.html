Talking to AI clearly

How to ask for what you want, in your own words, and improve the answer by asking again.

Sections complete: 0 of 4

Loading your progress…

An AI assistant is a tool you type to. You write what you need in ordinary words, and it writes something back. That is the whole interaction.

What it is good at: writing and rewriting text, explaining things in simpler words, summarising something long, translating, suggesting a formula, organising messy notes into a tidy list.

What it is not: it is not a search engine that always returns true facts, and it is not a colleague who knows your company. It does not know your customers, your policies, or last week's meeting unless you tell it. It has no memory of your work.

Keep this in mind

The AI produces text that sounds right. Sounding right and being right are two different things. You stay the person who decides what is correct.

A useful comparison

Think of it as a very fast assistant who writes well, has read a great deal, has never worked at your company, and will never tell you when it is unsure. That mix explains both why it saves you time and why you always read what it gives you.

You do not need perfect English to use AI. This is the part most people get wrong, and it stops them before they start.

Write the way you would speak to a colleague. Short sentences are fine. Spelling mistakes are fine. Mixing in words from your own language is fine. The AI will still understand you.

These all work

  • “pls write mail to vendor, delivery late, need update by friday”
  • “I need email for team about new timing of standup. keep short.”
  • “explain this to me simple, I don't understand”

None of these are polished English. All of them will get you a usable draft.

It works the other way too

You can also ask the AI to improve your English. Write your rough version, then ask: “fix the grammar and make this sound professional.” Over time you will notice the corrections and pick them up.

Waiting until your written English improves is the one approach that does not work, because using AI is one of the fastest ways to improve it.

A vague request gets a vague answer. There is a simple formula that fixes this, and it has three parts:

  1. Who you are — your role or situation.
  2. What you want — the actual task.
  3. How you want it — length, tone, format.

See the difference

WeakStrong
“Email.” “I'm an admin. Write a short, polite email to a vendor asking for a delivery update by Friday.”
“Write something for work.” “I run office facilities. Write a notice to all staff that the lift will be under repair on Saturday. Friendly tone, under 80 words.”
“Do the needful.” “I'm handling training records. Summarise the attached list into three bullet points I can read out in a meeting.”

The strong versions are not longer because long is better. They are longer because each extra piece removes a guess the AI would otherwise make for you.

The “how” part is the one people skip

Saying “as a bulleted list”, “under 100 words”, or “in a table” saves you the most rework. Ask for the shape you want.

The first answer is a draft, not a final result. Treat it as the start of a short conversation.

If something is not right, say so in plain words and send it again. You do not need to rewrite your original request or start a new chat.

Follow-up instructions that work

  • “make it shorter”
  • “more polite”  /  “less formal”
  • “put it in a table”
  • “remove the last paragraph”
  • “this sounds too stiff — write it like I'm talking to a colleague”
  • “give me three options so I can pick”

Two or three rounds of this usually gets you further than trying to write one perfect request at the start.

Try this today

Take a message you already need to write. Ask for a draft. Then send exactly one follow-up: “make it shorter and warmer.” Compare the two versions. That one round is the whole skill of Description in miniature.
